# Blending Combat Into a Skill Challenge

## Never stat a fight blind

The single hard rule: don't suggest enemy numbers, statblocks, or "this should be a tough/easy fight" difficulty calls until you know the party's tier or level. If the user hasn't said, ask before writing any combat-capable node's specifics. You can draft everything else — the chase structure, the DCs, the complications — while that answer is pending; just flag clearly in the file (and your response) which parts are waiting on it.

Once you have the tier, assume **3–4 party members** unless told otherwise. That's a meaningfully smaller action-economy than a "standard" 4-person adventuring party built for a dedicated combat encounter, especially if this fight is breaking out mid-chase when the party may be split up, out of position, or already spent resources on skill checks — factor that into how many enemies or how tough a single enemy should be. A fight that would be balanced as a fresh, focused combat encounter can run hot when it ambushes a party mid-scene.

Don't invent precise statblocks from scratch inside the encounter file unless asked — point to the tier-appropriate CR/level of monster or NPC stat block that fits the narrative role (a couple of "thug"-tier opponents, a single tougher one, an improvised-weapon-wielding non-combatant) and let the DM pull or reskin an actual statblock at the table. Naming the *role* and rough *toughness* is usually more useful during design than a fully-statted block that won't survive contact with what the party actually rolled.

## Combat is a possible outcome, not a mode switch

Resist writing "Phase 1: Skill Challenge" followed by "Phase 2: Combat" as two separate, sequential halves of the file. That structure quietly tells the DM combat is scheduled, which defeats the point. Instead:

- Attach combat as one of several possible **outcomes** of specific nodes or triggers, alongside "they get away," "the party catches them without a fight," etc.
- Let the party's own choices create combat too, not just failed rolls — someone can choose to tackle the quarry rather than out-run them, which is itself a trigger, independent of any check.
- If a trigger you and the user identified (a story beat, a threshold) makes combat *possible* rather than mandatory, say so explicitly in the node: "from here, combat becomes a live option — but the quarry would still rather run than fight" (or the reverse, if that's the read on them).

## Partial escalations

Real scenes rarely jump cleanly from "no violence" to "full combat." Build in the middle states:

- **A weapon appears** (grabbed, improvised, drawn) without anyone attacking yet — this is a great place for a DC check (calm the situation down, disarm, back off) that can still avoid a fight.
- **A hostage or bystander gets pulled in** — changes the shape of any combat that follows (can't AOE, has to isolate a threat) without necessarily starting one.
- **One combatant breaks off to fight while others keep fleeing** — lets a scene stay mixed, chase *and* combat at once, rather than resolving to one or the other for the whole party.

Write these as their own small node outcomes with a DC to de-escalate and a clear (but not certain) path to full combat if that DC is missed or nobody tries it.

## Sizing the trigger itself

When a scene has a scripted escalation point (a world-event landing, a scheduled arrival, anything not driven by dice) — treat it as a hard trigger that happens at a specific node or point in the clock regardless of rolls, but still don't force combat immediately after it. It should raise the ceiling on what's possible (loud opponents, previously-unavailable weapons, an environment that's suddenly chaotic) rather than instantly cutting to initiative. Give the party at least one beat to react to the new situation before anything is forced.
